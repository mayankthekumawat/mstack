# Applying the taste

These are invented examples for calibration. Start with [the actual videos, presentations, and paired scripts](source-work.md). The examples below are not excerpts from that work, measured results, or fixed templates. Follow the user's brief when it calls for a different approach.

## Give a presentation a question worth answering

Suppose the task is to explain a new internal search tool.

A generic opening lists retrieval, indexing, and model capabilities. An opening with a reason to listen asks:

> The document exists. Why are three people still helping you find the answer?

Follow one realistic request. Show where it stalls, what the new workflow changes, and the source behind the answer. Then explain the technical mechanism. Return to the opening by showing which handoffs remain and why.

The question works only if the supplied evidence supports the problem. If the actual bottleneck is conflicting instructions rather than search, change the question and demonstration to match it.

## Make a reveal change the explanation

For a talk about delivery delays, keep the same request visible while adding the steps that explain its journey.

| Beat | Narration's job | Screen's job |
|---|---|---|
| Establish the situation | Describe the customer's request. | Show the request and its intended recipient. |
| Expose the handoff | Explain who must act next. | Add that actor and the connection. |
| Locate the delay | Explain the constraint supported by the records. | Mark the waiting point in the same diagram. |
| Explain the intervention | Describe what the proposed change affects. | Change the relevant connection and retain the rest. |
| Test the payoff | State what the evidence does and does not establish. | Show the supported comparison and any remaining dependency. |

The sequence helps the audience locate a cause. Animating five unrelated bullet points would not do the same work.

For a reading deck, consolidate the sequence into an annotated diagram with enough context to understand the finding. Keep intermediate states only when they make the document easier to read.

## Adapt the same finding to another room

Assume records show that staff re-enter the same information in several systems. The amount of time saved by a proposed integration is unknown.

For a workshop, an opening might be:

> Same details. Third form. At this point the keyboard knows your address.

Show the repeated fields, explain why the systems ask for them, and demonstrate the proposed change. The aside creates recognition. It supplies no evidence of savings.

For a management report, write:

> Staff enter the same details in several systems. The proposed integration would reuse those fields. A pilot is needed to measure the effect on processing time and check how corrections reach each system.

Both versions describe the friction, explain the change, and preserve the missing measurement. The difference is the register.

## Plan a video as narration and visible action

For a character-led explainer, give a simple figure a concrete goal. Suppose a fictional customer tries to correct a delivery address. The character visits several desks, each of which sends the request elsewhere. Repeating the journey can establish a comic pattern while the accumulating route shows the cost of each handoff.

Then expose the mechanism: which system holds the address, who can change it, and how the correction reaches the delivery team. Revisit the same character after explaining a proposed change. Finish with what happens to that original request, including any remaining constraint. A callback can supply both the joke and the analytical payoff.

This is an invented storyboard prompted by the user's endorsement of character-led video. It is not a description of the source video's animation. A stick figure is an available storytelling device, not a required mascot for every artifact.

For an explainer about a counterintuitive research result, begin with a situation that makes the question tangible. Show the method before asking viewers to accept the result.

| Narration | Visual decision | Evidence constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Pose the question through a familiar situation. | Stage or draw that situation. | Label a reenactment when viewers could mistake it for recorded evidence. |
| Explain what the study compared. | Keep the groups and conditions visible. | Preserve the actual study design. |
| Introduce a technical term. | Show the behavior or relationship the term names. | Simplification must retain the meaning. |
| Explain the result and its limits. | Show the relevant comparison with readable labels. | Keep sample, units, and uncertainty where they matter. |
| Return to the opening. | Revisit the situation with the new explanation. | Do not generalize beyond the study's scope. |

Add an aside or visual callback only where it helps the intended audience follow the idea. Review sound, transitions, and timing in the finished video before claiming those aspects work.
